## For ages 8–11

This one is not really about luck — it is about **maths**, and once you see the maths you cannot un-see it.

A gambling game is built so that, on average, the people playing it lose and the company running it wins. That is not bad luck or a mean streak; it is a rule the company **decided in advance and wrote down.** They keep a small slice of everything that gets bet. You might win sometimes — that is what keeps it exciting — but play long enough and the slice always wins.

At your age the version to watch for is on a screen: games where you pay (or watch an advert) to open a "mystery box" or spin for a prize. They are training wheels for exactly this feeling. Knowing the trick — *the game was built so the maker comes out ahead* — is the whole defence. You do not have to be strong; you just have to see it.

## For ages 11–16

The most dangerous gambling products are the ones **on your phone**, and they are designed that way on purpose: fast, endless, always open, a new bet every few seconds. Speed and never stopping are what make a product hard to put down, and plenty of people your age already gamble.

Here is the part nobody can argue with, and why this one needs no complicated science:

- **The "house edge" is published.** The *house edge* is the share of every pound bet that a game is built to keep, on average, over time. On a roulette wheel it is just under 3p of every £1. For games like roulette or an online slot, the company states it in the rules or the terms and conditions. It is a guarantee, not a guess.
- So the honest line is: **you are not unlucky — you are on the wrong side of a number the company chose in advance, wrote down, and published.** Nobody has to cheat you. The edge is in the terms and conditions.

What you do now matters because the habit, and the harm, follow the *product*, not your personality — the faster and more non-stop the game, the more people it hurts. The defence is not out-willpowering a well-built machine; it is declining to play a game whose maths you have now seen.

## For ages 16+

The maths needs no statistics to prove it. (Scientists usually use a *p-value* to judge whether a pattern in their data could just be down to chance. Here there is nothing to judge — nobody is estimating anything from data.) The average loss per bet — the product's negative *expected value* — is a **published design setting**, not a research finding. The operator chose it in advance and printed it in the terms. Play long enough and the ups and downs of luck (the *variance*) even out; the edge does not.

On top of that sits a second, weaker claim that does need research — that the resulting harm is large and follows the product, not the person — and it is graded honestly in the evidence below: harm is measurably higher for the fastest, most non-stop products (online slots) than for slower ones (sports betting), the same way the harm from a cigarette depends on how much tar it delivers, not on who smokes it.

How today affects later: in bank data, the more of their income people spent on gambling, the more likely they were to fall into money trouble — and the very heaviest gamblers were much more likely to die early. Teenagers already gamble. The most protective move comes first — recognising that the machine's edge is fixed and published, and not opening the app in the first place. The claims that gambling *causes* debt, relationship breakdown and suicide are real but tangled up with other causes such as poverty and mental illness; the maths is not tangled up with anything.

## The evidence

*This part is the same for every age: it is where the facts above come from. Each study is described in plain words first, then named so you can look it up. Stuck on a word? See [Words we use](/words/).*

### Tier 1 — maths, not statistics

No statistics needed. The house edge — the share of every bet a game keeps on average — is set by whoever designs the game, not measured by researchers:

- Single-zero roulette: house edge **2.7%**. There are 37 numbers, but a win pays as if there were only 36, so over time the casino keeps 1/37 of all the money bet. Double-zero roulette: **5.26%** (2/38). (Standard game rules.)
- UK online slots: the *return-to-player* — the share of money bet that gets paid back out as prizes, on average — is typically **92–96%**. So the machine is set to keep 4–8% of everything staked, and the operator publishes it.
- The National Lottery pays out roughly **half** of the money spent on tickets as prizes.

These are guarantees, not tendencies. Play long enough and the ups and downs of luck even out; the edge does not. **You are not unlucky — you are on the wrong side of a number the company chose in advance, wrote down, and published.** Nobody has to lie to you. The edge is in the terms and conditions.

### Tier 2 — the research

- **How many people gamble, worldwide.** A major review pooling 299 prevalence studies found that in the past year **46.2% of adults** and **17.9% of adolescents** had gambled, and **10.3% of adolescents** had gambled online. Risky gambling (any level of gambling problem) affected **5.5% of women and 11.9% of men** — roughly **448.7 million adults** worldwide. Net losses are projected to reach about **US$700 billion by 2028**, and gambling is legal in more than 80% of countries. *Systematic review and meta-analysis*: **Lancet Public Health Commission on Gambling (Wardle et al., [Lancet Public Health 2024](<https://doi.org/10.1016/S2468-2667(24)00167-1>))**, drawing on its companion meta-analysis, **Tran et al., [Lancet Public Health 2024;9:e594](<https://doi.org/10.1016/S2468-2667(24)00126-9>)**.
- **Harm depends on the product.** Problem gambling (gambling that has got out of control and is causing harm) affected **15.8% of online casino/slot players** but **8.9% of sports bettors** (Wardle et al. 2024). Like the harm from a cigarette depending on how much tar it delivers, gambling harm follows the *design* of the product — how fast it is, how non-stop, betting during a match — not the character of the player.
- **What people really spent.** Researchers looked at the actual bank transactions of 6.5 million UK customers. The more of their income people spent on gambling, the more likely they were to fall into money trouble, and the very heaviest gamblers were much more likely to die early. This does not prove gambling *caused* it, but it measures real spending rather than what people say they spent — which is rare in this field. **Muggleton et al., [Nat Hum Behav 2021;5:319](<https://doi.org/10.1038/s41562-020-01045-w>)**.
- **Suicide.** Swedish national records show people with gambling disorder die by suicide at roughly **15-fold** (fifteen times) the usual rate — **Karlsson & Håkansson, [J Behav Addict 2018](<https://doi.org/10.1556/2006.7.2018.112>)**. Other things, such as depression or poverty, may explain part of this — but they rarely produce a gap that large.

## The honest weakness

It is hard to prove that gambling itself *causes* the later harms — debt, broken relationships, suicide — because poverty and mental illness are tangled up with gambling in both directions: they can lead to gambling, and gambling can lead to them (researchers call this *confounding*). The best evidence comes from *natural experiments* (also called quasi-experiments) — real-world rule changes that let us compare before and after: Norway's 2007 removal of slot machines, and the UK's 2019 cut of the maximum stake on fixed-odds betting terminals from £100 to £2. Note also what is deliberately *not* claimed here: any figure for the share of industry money that comes from people being harmed by gambling — the idea is sound, but the numbers going around trace back to campaigning groups rather than independent research.
