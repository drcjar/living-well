## For ages 8–11

Carrying a lot of extra body fat is genuinely bad for you. As you get older it makes serious illnesses — diabetes, heart disease, even some cancers — much more likely. That is not about how you look; it is about what the extra fat does inside your body.

Here is the hard part, and the reason grown-ups make such a fuss about it early: **once your body gets used to carrying extra weight, it fights to keep it.** Losing it later and staying that way turns out to be really difficult for almost everyone. So by far the easiest time to deal with it is to *not get there in the first place*.

The good news is that this is much less about willpower than it sounds. **You eat more when more food is easy to reach.** If there is a bowl of crisps next to you, you eat crisps; if there is a bowl of apples, you eat apples. And you eat more when you are simply *given* more — a bigger plate or a bigger packet makes almost everyone eat more without noticing. So the trick is not fighting yourself at the table — it is what ends up in the cupboard, and how much lands on the plate. That is what "eat food, mostly plants, not too much" is really about.

## For ages 11–16

Two blunt facts first, because they are the point:

- **Excess body fat causes disease.** It is not just about looks — higher body fat really does *cause* a higher risk of type 2 diabetes, heart disease and several cancers, and shortens life on average. This is one of the better-proven things on the whole site: studies using genes show it is cause and effect, not just two things that happen to go together.
- **The biggest risk factor for carrying excess weight later is carrying it now.** Weight tends to stay with you from childhood into adulthood, and once the body has gained fat it *defends* it — after weight loss, the body burns less energy and the weight tends to come back. Losing it is genuinely hard; not gaining it is far easier.

Put those together and the useful move is obvious: **don't get there in the first place** — and that is far more about your surroundings than your willpower. The amount you eat is partly down to the food itself: in the best experiment on this, the same people ate a lot more when the food was processed, without trying to. It is also down to *how much is put in front of you* — give people bigger portions and they reliably eat more, without feeling any fuller. So the levers you actually control come before you feel hungry — what is in the kitchen, what is easiest to grab, and how big the serving is (a smaller plate, not seconds by habit). Change the defaults and the eating changes with them.

## For ages 16+

Start with the part that is strong, because it is unusually strong for nutrition. **Excess body fat (adiposity) causes type 2 diabetes and heart disease** — Mendelian-randomisation studies, which compare people by genes fixed at birth, show this is cause and effect rather than something else hiding behind it (confounding). The WHO's cancer agency (IARC) judged that excess body fat **causes at least a dozen cancers**. And the risk of dying rises with BMI above the mid-20s. So "why it matters" is not in doubt.

The other strong, and under-appreciated, fact is about **reversibility**: the body defends a fat mass once it is reached. After substantial weight loss, the body keeps burning less energy at rest and the weight is usually regained; and obesity tracks powerfully from childhood — a child with obesity is roughly five times as likely to carry it into adulthood, and the great majority of adolescents with obesity stay that way. The practical upshot is stark: **the biggest risk factor for carrying excess weight is having carried it already.** Prevention beats reversal by a wide margin, which is why the leverage is early and in the environment.

Two environmental levers on *how much* you eat have unusually clean experimental support: how **processed** the food is (people eat more of it, unprompted) and how **large the portion** is (serve more and people eat more, with no matching rise in fullness). Both are about changing defaults, not out-arguing appetite — a smaller plate and a less-processed cupboard do work that willpower rarely wins.

Where the evidence is *weak* is the next question — *which named diet* to prescribe. Michael Pollan's *eat food, mostly plants, not too much* works less as a proven prescription than as a rule about **what to buy**. So the honest position is: the stakes are high and well-evidenced, and the defaults governing how much you eat are worth changing — but no specific named diet is a proven fix, and using smaller portions as a deliberate weight-*loss* tool meets the same defended-mass wall as everything else, so the effect on intake is far better evidenced than any lasting effect on the scale.

## The evidence

*This part is the same for every age: it is where the facts above come from. Each study is described in plain words first, then named so you can look it up. Stuck on a word? See [Words we use](/words/).*

### Why excess weight matters (the strong part)

- **Genes show it is cause and effect.** Some people inherit gene variants that make them carry a bit more body fat, through no choice of their own. Comparing people by those genes, higher body fat *causes* more coronary heart disease and type 2 diabetes, and fat carried around the waist also raises the risk of stroke — and because genes are fixed at birth, lifestyle or money cannot explain the link away. *Mendelian randomisation*: **Dale et al., [Circulation 2017;135:2373](<https://doi.org/10.1161/CIRCULATIONAHA.116.026560>)**.
- **Thirteen cancers.** Experts at the WHO's cancer agency reviewed all the evidence and concluded that not carrying excess body fat lowers the risk of thirteen cancers — in other words, excess body fat causes them. **Lauby-Secretan et al. (IARC Working Group), [NEJM 2016;375:794](<https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27557308/>)**.
- **The risk of dying rises with weight above a healthy range.** Pooling the records of about 3.9 million adults, the risk of dying was lowest at a BMI of 20–25 (BMI compares weight with height) and rose with overweight and obesity — about **31%** higher for every 5 BMI points (5 kg/m²) above 25. **Global BMI Mortality Collaboration, [Lancet 2016;388:776](<https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(16)30175-1>)**.

### Why prevention beats reversal

- **The body fights to keep weight on.** Six years after contestants on the TV show *The Biggest Loser* lost a lot of weight, their bodies were still burning fewer calories at rest than their new size would predict, and 13 of the 14 had put weight back on. **Fothergill et al., [Obesity 2016;24:1612](<https://doi.org/10.1002/oby.21538>)**.
- **Weight in childhood tends to stay.** Pooling studies that followed children into adulthood, children with obesity were around five times as likely to have obesity as adults, and roughly **80%** of adolescents with obesity still had it as adults. **Simmonds et al., [Obes Rev 2016;17:95](<https://doi.org/10.1111/obr.12334>)**.

### How much, and what, you eat

- **Processed food makes people eat more, without trying.** In the most carefully controlled eating experiment in nutrition, 20 adults lived in a research unit and ate two diets for two weeks each, in random order: one ultra-processed, one unprocessed. The meals offered were matched for calories, sugar, fat, fibre and other nutrients, and people could eat as much as they liked. On the ultra-processed diet they ate about **500 kcal/day** more and gained weight; on the unprocessed diet they lost it. How much they ate depended on the food, not on the person. *Randomised crossover trial*: **Hall et al., [Cell Metab 2019;30:67](<https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cmet.2019.05.008>)**.
- **Bigger portions make people eat more.** A review of experiments found people consistently choose and eat more when they are given bigger portions, bigger packets, or bigger plates and glasses. Serving size changes how much we eat, whatever our willpower. **Hollands et al. (Cochrane), [portion, package and tableware size, 2015](<https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26368271/>)**.
- **…without feeling fuller.** In a controlled feeding study, bigger portions made both normal-weight and overweight people eat more, and they did not report feeling any fuller. (This helps explain how weight is gained; using smaller portions to *lose* weight runs into the body's fight-back described above.) **Rolls et al., [Am J Clin Nutr 2002;76:1207](<https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12450884/>)**.
- **Diet and deaths worldwide.** The Global Burden of Disease study estimates roughly **11 million deaths per year** are linked to poor diets — mostly too much salt, too few whole grains and too little fruit: the "mostly plants" part. **GBD 2017 Diet Collaborators, [Lancet 2019;393:1958](<https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(19)30041-8>)**.
- **More fruit and veg keeps helping.** Pooling studies, the benefit of fruit and vegetables keeps rising up to about **800 g/day** — roughly ten portions. **Aune et al., [Int J Epidemiol 2017](<https://doi.org/10.1093/ije/dyw319>)**.
- **The best diet trial.** In PREDIMED, a Spanish trial, people given a Mediterranean diet (with extra olive oil or nuts) had about **30%** fewer heart attacks, strokes and heart deaths than a comparison group. The trial was retracted and re-published after problems with how people were put into groups — strong, but not spotless. **PREDIMED (Estruch et al., [NEJM 2018](<https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa1800389>))**.

## The honest weakness

Keep two things separate. That excess body fat *causes* serious disease is well-evidenced — the gene studies and the IARC review put it beyond the usual "something else explains it" complaints. What is weak is the *diet advice*: most nutrition studies rely on people remembering and reporting what they ate, which is unreliable; people who eat differently also differ in money, jobs and much else, which muddles the results (confounding); and findings often fail to repeat. The Hall experiment is the honourable exception, and it is small (n=20, four weeks). So state the stakes plainly, but do not oversell any single named diet as the fix — which is why the page's confidence is only moderate.
