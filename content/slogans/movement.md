## For ages 8–11

This is the cheerful one. You do not need a gym, and you do not need to hit any special number.

Here is the real finding, and it is good news: the **biggest** health boost from moving comes simply from *not being one of the people who barely moves at all.* Going from almost-no-movement to some-movement is where nearly all the benefit is. After that, more is a nice bonus, not a rule.

So whatever gets you off the sofa counts — running around, cycling, a sport, dancing, walking the dog. Getting up and moving *at all* is the win.

And the best bit for right now: moving your body actually makes you feel good *today* — a better mood, a clearer head, less grumpiness. You don't have to wait fifty years to get something out of it.

## For ages 11–16

The science here is genuinely encouraging:

- The single biggest gain of all comes from **moving out of the least-active group.** If you do almost nothing now, a little regular movement buys you more than a very fit person gains by training even harder.
- You need **less than most people think.** Most of the benefit arrives well before any big daily target.

So there is nothing you have to "hit" and no membership to buy. Whatever movement you can stick with — a sport, a walk, a bike ride — gets you most of what matters. What you build now is a habit that keeps paying off for decades, but even judged on today it is the most winnable item on this whole site.

And the payoff is not only decades away. Regular activity is one of the most reliable ways to lift a low mood and take the edge off stress and anxiety — something you feel in days and weeks, not decades. There is even genetic evidence that being more active *causes* lower rates of depression, not just that cheerful people happen to move more.

One concrete, free option worth knowing about: **parkrun** — a free, weekly, timed 5 km run *or walk* in a local park, open to anyone and deliberately welcoming to complete beginners (plenty of people walk it). It rolls the two benefits into one cheap package: movement, plus a bit of outdoor social contact. It is a neat example of the whole idea — not "try harder," but an easy, low-cost option that makes the good choice easy.

## For ages 16+

Narrowly: physical activity lowers the risk of dying early; the benefit is largest for people moving *out of the least active* group; and the amount that captures most of it is far below the round numbers in circulation.

The more you move, the lower the risk — but the benefit is **front-loaded**: the largest drop in risk comes from leaving the least-active quarter of people, measured with movement trackers (accelerometers) so it isn't inflated by people over-reporting. Getting off the floor matters far more than optimising the top.

How today affects later: the obvious worry is reverse causation — ill people move less, which would make movement look better than it is. Mendelian-randomisation work, which compares people by genes linked to how active they are, points to a genuine protective effect. Do not oversell the top of the curve, where other differences between very active and less active people muddy the picture; the claim is strongest exactly where it matters most for a population — at the bottom, where the effect is largest and the cause-and-effect evidence best.

And the benefit is not only on the seventy-year clock. Physical activity is one of the best-evidenced near-term levers on mood, reliably easing symptoms of depression and anxiety over weeks — and a Mendelian-randomisation study supports activity *causing* lower depression risk, since genes are fixed at birth and cannot be explained away by "happier people move more." That near-term effect on how you feel is why this page sits on both clocks.

## The evidence

*This part is the same for every age: it is where the facts above come from. Each study is described in plain words first, then named so you can look it up. Stuck on a word? See [Words we use](/words/).*

- **The official guidelines.** The World Health Organization recommends 150–300 minutes a week of moderate activity for adults (activity that makes you warmer and breathe harder), and an average of 60 minutes a day for 5–17-year-olds. Since 2020 there is no minimum length for a session: all movement counts. **WHO 2020 guidelines (Bull et al., [Br J Sports Med 2020;54:1451](<https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2020-102955>))**.
- **Measured, not guessed.** Pooling studies of about 36,000 adults who wore movement trackers (accelerometers) — so nobody could over-report — the biggest drop in the risk of dying came from moving out of the least-active quarter. Getting off the sofa matters far more than training harder at the top end. *Meta-analysis*: **Ekelund et al., [BMJ 2019;366:l4570](<https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.l4570>)**.
- **Steps.** Pooling step-count studies of about 47,000 adults, most of the benefit came at a daily step count well below the popular round numbers, and then levelled off — more is not proportionally better. **Paluch et al., [Lancet Public Health 2022;7:e219](<https://doi.org/10.1016/S2468-2667(21)00302-9>)**.
- **Is it really cause and effect?** The obvious worry is the reverse: people who are ill move less. Studies using genes linked to how active people are (the genes were found by **Klimentidis et al., [Int J Obes 2018](<https://doi.org/10.1038/s41366-018-0120-3>)**) point to a genuine protective effect, making the ordinary studies more believable.
- **Mood.** Using the same gene-based method (*Mendelian randomisation*), **Choi et al., [JAMA Psychiatry 2019;76:399](<https://doi.org/10.1001/jamapsychiatry.2018.4175>)** found that being more active, measured by trackers, *causes* a lower risk of major depression — and found no clear sign of the reverse, that depression itself makes people less active. That is unusually strong evidence for a benefit you feel now, alongside many experiments showing activity improves mood and eases anxiety over weeks.
- **A concrete, free example — parkrun.** Interviews with parkrunners found that what gets people started and keeps them coming is that it is free, open to all and friendly, and that people help and support each other — a template for easy community activity. *Qualitative study*: **Stevinson et al., [Int J Behav Med 2015;22:170](<https://doi.org/10.1007/s12529-014-9431-5>)**. Honest caveat — **Haake et al., [Health Promot Int 2022;37](<https://doi.org/10.1093/heapro/daab138>)**: people in better-off areas take part far more (one well-off Sheffield parkrun drew about seven times as many people as one in a deprived area), so just providing it is not enough for everyone to benefit equally.

## The honest weakness

At the top end — people doing 300 minutes a week — the results are muddied: very active people differ from inactive people in lots of ways that questionnaires don't capture (confounding), and adjusting for it doesn't fix that. The claim is strongest exactly where it matters most for a population — at the bottom, among people who barely move — where the effect is largest and the gene-based evidence best. Do not oversell the top end. On mood: the cause-and-effect evidence is real but clearest for activity *measured by trackers*, and the effect is modest — a reliable lift, not a cure. And community options like parkrun are promising rather than proven — the evidence comes from people who chose to turn up (so they may already be more motivated), and uptake is higher in better-off areas — so treat it as a good option to make available, not a guaranteed fix.
