# Words we use

<p class="lede">The evidence on each page uses some science words. Here is what they mean, in plain English.</p>

## The two clocks

**Ten-year clock** — the next ten years of your life. A risk on the ten-year clock is one that could actually affect you soon, while you are growing up — and something you can do something about now.

**Seventy-year clock** — the whole of your life ahead, roughly the next seventy years. A risk on the seventy-year clock builds up slowly, like the damage from smoking, and mostly shows up decades later.

## Kinds of study

**Case-control study** — researchers take people who already have an illness (the "cases") and similar people who don't (the "controls"), and compare what they did in the past. Quick and cheap, but it relies on people remembering the past accurately. *Example: the 1950 smoking study.*

**Cohort study** — researchers follow a large group of people (a "cohort") forward over years, recording what they do and what happens to them. Slower, but it records things as they happen rather than relying on memory. *Example: the British Doctors Study, which followed doctors for 50 years.*

**Randomised controlled trial (RCT)** — people are split into groups *by chance*, like a coin toss, and each group gets something different. Because chance decided who got what, the groups start out alike, so a difference at the end is very likely caused by the thing being tested. The strongest kind of study, but rare for everyday habits. *Example: the Nambour sunscreen trial.*

**Crossover trial** — everyone tries both options, one after the other, in a random order, so each person is compared with themselves. *Example: the processed-food experiment.*

**Natural experiment** (also called a *quasi-experiment*) — a real-world change that nobody set up for research, such as a new law, which lets researchers compare before and after, or one place with another. Not as clean as a randomised trial, but often the best we can get for big questions. *Example: Scotland's indoor smoking ban.*

**Mendelian randomisation** — using genes as a natural experiment. Some people are born with gene variants that nudge them towards, say, drinking less or carrying more body fat. Genes are shuffled at conception — before anyone's money, habits or health can affect them — so comparing people by their genes shows cause and effect more cleanly than ordinary comparisons. *Example: the alcohol study in China.*

**Systematic review** and **meta-analysis** — instead of trusting one study, researchers gather *every* good study on a question (a systematic review) and, where they can, combine the results into one overall answer (a meta-analysis). A **Cochrane review** is a particularly careful kind, from an independent international network of researchers.

**Qualitative study** — researchers interview people to understand *why* they do things, rather than counting how many do. *Example: the parkrun study.*

## Cause and effect

**Cause** (or **causal**) — when one thing actually makes another happen. That is different from two things simply going together (a **correlation** or **association**): ice-cream sales and sunburn rise together, but ice cream does not cause sunburn — sunny weather causes both.

**Confounding** — when a hidden third thing makes two others look linked. The sunny weather above is a *confounder*. Much of the hard work in health research is ruling confounders out.

**Reverse causation** — when the arrow points the other way from what you would assume. People who are ill tend to move less, so "moving less" can look as if it causes illness when partly it is the illness causing less movement.

**The sick-quitter problem** — in alcohol studies, the "non-drinkers" group used to include people who had stopped drinking *because* they were already ill. That made non-drinkers look unhealthy, and made light drinking look good by comparison.

## The numbers

**Relative risk (RR)** — how many times more (or less) likely something is in one group than another. An RR of 2 means twice as likely; 1 means no difference; 0.5 means half as likely. On the smoking page, stop-smoking medicines have RRs above 1 — they make quitting *more* likely.

**Hazard ratio (HR)** — very like a relative risk, but it compares how often something happens over a period of time. An HR of 0.5 means it happened about half as often in one group as in the other.

**Confidence interval (CI)** — the range the true answer probably lies within, given the data (a "95% CI" is worked out so that, 95 times out of 100, it would contain the true answer). A narrow range means a precise answer; a wide one means we are unsure. If the range for an RR or HR includes 1, the study cannot rule out "no difference at all".

**Percentage points** — the gap between two percentages. Going from 10% to 8% is a fall of 2 *percentage points* — but a fall of 20% compared with where it started.

**p-value** — a number scientists use to judge whether a pattern in their data could just be down to chance. It says how often you would see a pattern at least that strong if nothing were really going on; a small p-value means chance alone would rarely produce it. The gambling maths does not need one, because nothing there is estimated from data.

**Effect size** — how big a difference something makes, not just *whether* it makes one.

**Absolute and relative risk** — relative risk says how much a risk is multiplied; absolute risk says how big the risk actually is. Doubling a one-in-a-million risk still leaves it tiny. That is why "no safe dose" of alcohol and "one drink adds only a small risk" can both be true.

**Front-loaded** — when most of the benefit comes from the first bit. On the movement page, going from barely moving to moving a little gives the biggest gain.

## Gambling words

**House edge** — the share of every pound bet that a game is built to keep, on average, over time. Single-zero roulette has a house edge of just under 3p in every £1.

**Return-to-player (RTP)** — the other side of the house edge: the share of the money bet that gets paid back out as prizes, on average. A machine that pays back 94p of every £1 keeps the other 6p.

**Expected value** — what you would win or lose *on average* per bet if you played many, many times. For gambling products it is always a loss for the player: that loss is the house edge.

**Variance** — the ups and downs of luck around that average. Luck can make you win tonight; over many bets it evens out. The house edge does not.

## Health words

**Acute** — sudden and short-lived, like an injury or alcohol poisoning. The opposite is **chronic**: long-lasting, like heart disease.

**Carcinogen, and "Group 1"** — a carcinogen is something that causes cancer. The World Health Organization's cancer agency (IARC) puts things it is sure cause cancer in humans on its **Group 1** list — tobacco, alcohol, sunlight and sunbeds are all on it. Group 1 tells you how *sure* we are that something causes cancer, not how *much* cancer it causes.

**Melanoma** — the most serious kind of skin cancer.

**BMI (body mass index)** — your weight compared with your height. A rough guide for adults; for children it is compared with others of the same age and sex.

**Obesity** — carrying enough excess body fat to harm health.

**Body clock** (or **circadian rhythm**) — your body's built-in 24-hour timer, which sets when you feel sleepy and when you feel alert. **Melatonin** is the hormone it uses to signal "time to sleep".

**Nicotine replacement therapy (NRT)** — patches, gum, lozenges and sprays that give nicotine without smoke, to ease cravings while quitting. **Varenicline** and **cytisine** are tablets that reduce cravings and make smoking less satisfying.

**CBT-I** — cognitive behavioural therapy for insomnia: a short course of therapy that changes sleep habits and the worries that keep you awake. The first treatment to try for long-lasting insomnia.

## Words about this site

**How sure** (confidence) — our overall judgement of how strong the evidence is on each page, from *near-certain* down to *contested*.

**Honest weakness** — the weakest point in the evidence for a page, stated on purpose. If we cannot name one, we have not finished reading.

**Provenance** — where something came from. The [provenance](/provenance/) page lists every number on the site with its source.
