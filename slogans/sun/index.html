Slip, slop, slap

The best-tested advice on this whole list — one of the very few everyday health tips checked by a proper randomised experiment.

How sure
High
Clock
Both clocks

Both clocks: this matters now, in the next ten years of your life, and over your whole life — roughly the next seventy years. More on the two clocks.

For ages 8–11

Slip on a shirt, slop on sunscreen, slap on a hat. Three little actions, one for each word — that is the whole thing, and it is a really good piece of advice.

Why is it good? Because it does not threaten you or blame you. It just tells you three easy things to do. And unlike a lot of health advice, this one was actually tested properly in a real experiment, and it worked: people who used sunscreen regularly got less skin cancer later on.

The nice part is how little effort it takes — a shirt, some sunscreen, a hat, and staying in the shade when the sun is strong. Small actions, real protection.

For ages 11–16

Most items on this site are about risks decades away. This one is different — it is already relevant to you. A serious skin cancer called melanoma is one of the commonest cancers in people aged 15–34 in the UK. So this is one of the rare cases where "now" and "in fifty years" point the same way.

Two things worth knowing:

  • The action is genuinely easy — sunscreen, a shirt, shade — and it is backed by a proper experiment where people were split into groups by chance, which is rarer than you'd think for this kind of advice.
  • Sunbeds are officially on the top list of things proven to cause cancer ("Group 1") — the same list as tobacco. A tan from a sunbed is selling you exactly what the evidence warns against.

What you do now shapes your future risk in a clear way here: sun damage adds up over a lifetime, and in the experiment the protection was still visible a decade after it ended. Small habit, lasting effect.

For ages 16+

Narrowly: regular sunscreen use reduces skin cancer, and — unusually for an everyday habit — this rests on a genuine randomised controlled trial rather than just observation. The line itself is a model of how to write health advice: three verbs, an action for each, no threat and no blame.

The Nambour trial randomly split a whole community's volunteers into daily versus use-when-you-like sunscreen, and ten years after the trial stopped the daily-sunscreen group had roughly half the melanomas. That the benefit outlasted the trial is the striking part, and it is why this is the strongest study design on the site.

How today affects later: melanoma is already among the commonest cancers in your age group, so this is one of the very few items where the ten-year and seventy-year clocks agree; and because damage builds up, the protective habit pays both now and later. Be honest about the limits, though — it is one trial, one fair-skinned, high-sunshine population, melanoma was not the main thing the trial was set up to measure (a secondary endpoint), and the invasive-melanoma result rests on very small numbers. Even the best study here does not deliver certainty.

The evidence

This part is the same for every age: it is where the facts above come from. Each study is described in plain words first, then named so you can look it up. Stuck on a word? See Words we use.

  • The Nambour Skin Cancer Prevention Trial (Queensland, Australia, 1992–96). 1,621 randomly chosen adults living in the town of Nambour were split by chance into two groups: one put sunscreen on every day, the other used it whenever they liked. At the same time they were also split to take either a beta-carotene supplement or a dummy pill — a 2×2 factorial design, which tests two things at once. A properly randomised trial of an everyday habit, in a whole community, barely exists anywhere else.
    • Green et al., Lancet 1999;354:723 — daily sunscreen reduced squamous cell carcinoma, a common skin cancer; there was no clear effect on basal cell carcinoma, another common but rarely dangerous one.
    • Green et al., J Clin Oncol 2011;29:257 — ten years after the trial ended: 11 melanomas in the daily-sunscreen group versus 22 in the other group (HR 0.50 — about half the rate; 95% CI 0.24–1.02). For invasive melanoma, the kind that has started to grow deeper into the skin: 3 versus 11 (HR 0.27, 95% CI 0.08–0.97). The difference was still there a decade after the trial stopped. (HR and CI are explained in Words we use.)
  • Why it matters to teenagers specifically: melanoma is one of the commonest cancers in 15–34-year-olds in the UK — one of the very few items where the ten-year and seventy-year clocks point the same way. Sunbeds are on IARC's Group 1 list (proven to cause cancer in humans).
  • Slip, slop, slap (Australia, 1981) is also the best-written public-health line of its kind: three verbs, an action you can copy for each, no threat, no blame, naming what to do rather than what might happen. Since then, melanoma rates have fallen among Australians born more recently while still rising in older generations — which fits the campaign having shaped the habits of people who grew up with it.

The honest weakness

One trial, one population (fair-skinned residents of sunny Queensland), and melanoma was not the main thing the trial was set up to measure. The range of likely results for melanoma overall (the confidence interval) just reaches 1.0 — the value that would mean "no difference". The invasive-melanoma result rests on small numbers — 3 versus 11 cases. It is the best study design here, and the answer is still imprecise — a reminder that even the strongest evidence on the site does not deliver certainty.
