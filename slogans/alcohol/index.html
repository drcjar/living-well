Alcohol: no safe dose

True — no amount of alcohol is good for you, and the risk starts with the first drink. But the extra risk from one drink is small. Say both halves, or people will stop believing you.

How sure
High
Clock
Both clocks

Both clocks: this matters now, in the next ten years of your life, and over your whole life — roughly the next seventy years. More on the two clocks.

For ages 8–11

You are too young to drink, so this page is not really about tonight — it is about one honest, slightly boring fact worth knowing early.

Here it is: alcohol is never actually good for your body. Not in a small amount, not in a large one. You will hear people say things like "a glass of wine is good for your heart" — that turned out to be a mistake in how the old studies were done. There is no amount that helps.

That does not mean a single drink will badly hurt an adult — usually it won't. It just means there is no magic safe level where alcohol switches from good to bad, because it was never on the good side to begin with.

For ages 11–16

Here is the thing the lifetime-cancer statistics miss: for someone your age, the risk from alcohol is tonight, not thirty years away. The real dangers are immediate and physical — injury, being hurt in a fight, drowning, choking, passing out, and things that get done to you or by you that cannot be taken back. That is a completely different, and much closer, kind of harm than liver disease decades from now.

One honest point to hold onto: there is no amount of alcohol that is good for you — the idea that "a glass of red wine is healthy" turned out to be a mistake in how the old studies were done. But be fair too: for an adult, the extra risk from one drink is small. The honest message is that the risk starts at the first drink and grows with every drink after it — not that one drink is deadly.

What you do now matters for later too — but the more useful message for you is the near one: the danger you can actually see coming is the same night, not decades away.

For ages 16+

Here is the claim in full, with its honest limits. "No safe dose" packs two separate facts together, and you need both — the scary half on its own would overstate the risk, and people would be right to stop listening.

  • The risk starts at the first drink. There is no low amount where the risk is zero, or where alcohol protects you. The old "J-curve" — the graph that seemed to show light drinkers had healthier hearts than non-drinkers — was a mistake: the "non-drinkers" it compared against included people who had stopped drinking because they were already ill, which made not drinking look worse than it is.
  • But the risk from a little is small. The extra risk from one drink a week is tiny. There is also no perfectly safe amount of cycling, or sunlight, or getting into a car. So the honest version keeps the truth and drops the exaggeration: there is no amount of alcohol that is good for you — there never was — and the risk starts at your first drink and rises with every one after it.

How today affects later: because there is no safe cut-off, risk builds up with the total you drink over your life, rather than waiting until you cross some "safe limit". What matters is the long-run pattern, not any single night — and the clearest example is breast cancer, where risk rises steadily with each daily drink, starting from the first (see the evidence).

The evidence

This part is the same for every age: it is where the facts above come from. Each study is described in plain words first, then named so you can look it up. Stuck on a word? See Words we use.

  • Why the "healthy glass of wine" idea fell apart. Researchers re-analysed 107 studies that had followed a total of 4.8 million people over time. The apparent benefit of light drinking disappeared once they corrected for a flaw in the older studies: their "non-drinker" groups included people who had stopped because they were already ill, and former heavy drinkers (the sick-quitter problem). Drinking less than 25 g/day (about three UK units) did not lower the risk of dying; the risk was clearly higher at ≥25 g/day in women and ≥45 g/day in men. The apparent heart benefit showed up only in older studies — the ones most likely to have that flaw. Zhao, Stockwell, Naimi et al., JAMA Netw Open 2023;6:e236185 (corrected May 2023 for errors in a figure's confidence intervals).
  • The decisive test used genes. Some people, mostly in East Asia, inherit gene variants (ALDH2 rs671 and ADH1B rs1229984) that make alcohol unpleasant, so they drink less — for a reason fixed at conception, not by their health or money. Among about 500,000 people in China, ordinary comparisons showed the familiar pattern where light drinkers seemed to have fewer strokes. But comparing people by their genes, stroke risk simply rose steadily with every extra drink. The apparent protection was caused by other differences between drinkers and non-drinkers. Mendelian randomisation: Millwood et al., Lancet 2019;393:1831 (China Kadoorie Biobank).
  • Officially a cause of cancer. The World Health Organization's cancer agency, IARC (Monograph 100E), has put alcoholic drinks in Group 1 — its list of things proven to cause cancer in humans — since 1988, and has since added acetaldehyde, the chemical the body makes when it breaks alcohol down. Alcohol causes cancers of the mouth, throat, voice box (larynx), food pipe (oesophagus), liver, bowel and female breast.
  • Breast cancer risk rises from the first drink. Pooling data from studies of women around the world, breast cancer risk rose about 7% per 10 g/day of alcohol (a little over one UK unit a day), with no amount low enough to show no effect — Collaborative Group on Hormonal Factors in Breast Cancer, Br J Cancer 2002. This is the clearest single example of "no safe dose": the risk starts rising from zero.
  • Official advice. The UK Chief Medical Officers 2016 say no level of drinking is completely safe, and advise adults to keep to no more than 14 units a week. Canada's Guidance on Alcohol and Health (CCSA 2023) went further, dropping the idea of a safe limit for a sliding scale (1–2 drinks a week low risk, 3–6 moderate, 7+ increasingly high).

The honest weakness

"No safe dose" tells you the direction of the risk — it only goes up — but not its size. There is also no perfectly safe amount of cycling, of sunlight, or of getting into a car. The extra risk from one drink a week is very small — so the honest version keeps the truth and drops the exaggeration: "There is no dose at which alcohol is good for you. There never was. The risk starts at your first drink and rises with every one after it."
