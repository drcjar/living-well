Smoking kills, stopping helps

The best-proven fact on this whole site: smoking kills — and stopping, at any age, gives years of life back. Each half has its own proof.

How sure
Near-certain
Clock
Both clocks

Both clocks: this matters now, in the next ten years of your life, and over your whole life — roughly the next seventy years. More on the two clocks.

For ages 8–11

Almost nothing you do at your age is likely to make you ill or hurt you badly — and when a child your age does get seriously ill, it is nearly always something nobody chose, like a rare illness or an accident. Smoking is not on that list yet. It is a slow danger, not a fast one.

So why talk about it now? Because of one strange fact: almost everyone who ends up smoking for life started when they were young — around your age or a little older. The people who sell cigarettes and vapes know this. That is why the danger is worth knowing about early, even though it feels far away.

Here is the good news, and it is the whole point: with smoking, not starting fixes everything. You do not have to be strong or give anything up. If you never start, the entire problem simply never happens to you. It is one of the very few big health choices where doing nothing is the winning move.

For ages 11–16

If you look at what actually harms people your age in this country, smoking is not near the top. The things most likely to seriously hurt or kill a teenager are accidents — especially on the roads — and self-harm. Cigarettes belong to a much slower clock: they mostly harm people decades from now.

But your teens are exactly when that slow clock gets started, and it is exactly when nicotine gets sold to you — now mostly as vapes. So two honest things at once:

  • Vaping nicotine is not the same as smoking. Cigarettes harm you by burning — the smoke is full of the chemicals that do the long-term damage. A vape does not burn, so it delivers far fewer of those chemicals. Anyone who tells you a vape is "just as bad as a cigarette" is wrong, and once you know that, you will stop trusting the rest of what they say. That is why being straight with you matters.
  • But for someone who has never smoked, starting to vape is pure loss. You would be taking on an addiction with nothing to trade for it — vapes have only existed since around 2007, so nobody can honestly promise you they are safe over a lifetime, because that lifetime hasn't happened yet.

What you do now shapes the whole rest of your life. Not starting removes almost the entire future risk, at no cost to you. It is the single most valuable health decision you can make — and it matters most right now, because this is exactly when the addiction is designed to catch you.

And if someone you love already smokes, this matters for them too: quitting is genuinely hard, but it is not just willpower. Proper help — medicines plus support — makes success far more likely, and it is never too late. They do not have to do it alone.

For ages 16+

Now you are old enough to weigh the long clock honestly, so here it is without softening.

Cigarettes are the most clearly proven cause of early death in all of medicine. Lifelong smokers die on average about ten years younger than people who never smoked, and roughly two in every three lifelong smokers are eventually killed by it. This is not a weak statistical hint; it is one of the most repeated findings in science, shown in different countries, in men and women, across decades, with the biological mechanism traced all the way down to the exact DNA damage. The details and sources are in the evidence below — check them.

But the second half is what turns a grim fact into a decision you control:

  • The risk is not banked. It is largely reversible if you get out early. Stopping before 40 avoids the large majority of the extra risk; stopping before 30 avoids almost all of it. Even stopping at 60 buys back years. There is no age at which quitting fails to help.
  • Starting is the only part of the story fully in your hands. Almost all lifelong smokers start before 20. Not starting removes the entire problem at no cost — which is why, if you have made it this far without smoking, you have already made the most valuable move.
  • And if you already smoke, stopping is not a coin-flip of willpower. Proper support — help from an adviser plus medication — and effective medicines (nicotine replacement, varenicline, cytisine) multiply the odds of success; the numbers are in the evidence below.

On vaping, the honest position is the same one as the younger band, just with the evidence attached: nicotine vaping delivers far fewer of the chemicals made by burning tobacco, and there is high-quality evidence it helps people who already smoke to quit — for a smoker, switching is a genuine gain. For a never-smoker none of that applies: it is addiction with no offsetting benefit and no long-term safety record. (The 2019–20 lung-injury scare, EVALI, was traced to vitamin E acetate in illegal cannabis (THC) cartridges, not nicotine vapes — getting that wrong is the fastest way to lose an argument you should win.)

The evidence

This part is the same for every age: it is where the facts above come from. Each study is described in plain words first, then named so you can look it up. Stuck on a word? See Words we use.

Half 1 — smoking kills

  • The first big clue (1950). Two researchers — a doctor, Richard Doll, and a statistician, Austin Bradford Hill — compared 649 hospital patients who had lung cancer with similar patients who did not, and asked them all about smoking. The lung cancer patients were far more likely to be smokers — the link was already impossible to miss. Case-control study: Doll & Hill, BMJ 1950;2:739.
  • Following 34,439 doctors for 50 years. Researchers tracked the same male doctors from 1951 onwards, and almost nobody was lost from the count. Doctors who smoked cigarettes died on average about 10 years younger than those who never smoked. Cohort study: British Doctors Study — Doll, Peto, Boreham & Sutherland, BMJ 2004;328:1519.
  • Checked again, elsewhere. A study of over a million British women (Pirie et al., Lancet 2013;381:133, the Million Women Study) and a large US national survey (Jha et al., NEJM 2013;368:341) each found the same roughly ten-year gap — in different countries, in women as well as men, decades later.
  • How many smokers it kills. A large Australian study found that about two in every three lifelong smokers die because of smoking — more than the older estimate of one in two. Banks et al., BMC Med 2015 (45 and Up Study).
  • How it does the damage. A chemical in tobacco smoke (benzo[a]pyrene), once the body has processed it, sticks to DNA at exactly the spots in the TP53 gene (codons 157, 248 and 273) that are most often broken in smokers' lung tumours. So the chain — smoke in, DNA damaged, tumour grows — has been traced step by step, not just guessed. Denissenko et al., Science 1996;274:430.
  • Where "how do we know it's cause and effect?" came from. Bradford Hill's famous checklist for deciding whether a link is really cause and effect was written about smoking. Bradford Hill, Proc R Soc Med 1965;58:295.

Half 2 — stopping helps

  • The earlier you stop, the more years you get back. In the British Doctors Study, men who stopped at 60, 50, 40 and 30 gained about 3, 6, 9 and 10 years of life respectively — Doll et al. 2004.
  • Stop young and you avoid almost all of it. In the Million Women Study, stopping before 40 avoided >90% of the extra risk of dying early; stopping before 30 avoided >97% — Pirie et al. 2013. The risk is not banked — it is largely reversible if you get out early.

The second half matters as much as the first: because the risk is largely reversible if you stop early, this is a reason to act, not a reason to give up.

Stopping is achievable — support and treatment work

Quitting is hard, but it is not a test of character you simply pass or fail. What multiplies success is proper support — help from an adviser plus medication, as NHS stop-smoking services provide — and effective medicines. The numbers below are relative risks (RR): how many times as likely people were to quit. RR 1.55, for example, means about half as likely again — Words we use explains more.

  • Medicine plus support beats trying alone. People given stop-smoking medicine and support from an adviser were much more likely to quit than those given only brief advice or usual care — Stead et al., Cochrane 2016 (RR 1.83). Adding extra support on top of medicine helps a bit more — Hartmann-Boyce et al., Cochrane 2019 (RR 1.15).
  • Nicotine replacement (patches, gum, lozenges) makes quitting about half as likely again — Hartmann-Boyce et al., Cochrane 2018 (RR 1.55).
  • Varenicline, a prescription tablet, more than doubles the chance, and cytisine, a similar tablet, also helps — Livingstone-Banks et al., Cochrane 2023 (varenicline RR 2.32; cytisine RR 1.30).
  • Comparing all the medicines at once, varenicline and combining two kinds of nicotine replacement (say, a patch plus gum) come out best — Cahill et al., Cochrane 2013.

On vaping

  • Vapes help smokers quit. Pooling the trials, there is strong ("high-certainty") evidence that nicotine vapes help people who already smoke to stop — better than nicotine patches or gum — Cochrane (Lindson et al., 2024). For a smoker, switching is a gain; for a never-smoker the argument does not apply at all. Vapes deliver far lower levels of the burning chemicals behind the ~10-year loss of life, and are not the same as smoking — but nobody can have 30-year safety data for a product that only appeared around 2007.

The honest weakness

There is essentially no honest weakness on the core claim: the evidence that smoking kills is about as strong as evidence in medicine gets, and the open questions are about policy, not about whether it is true. The one thing to keep straight is that the ~10-year figure is about burning tobacco — it does not carry over wholesale to vaping, which is a different and far newer thing.
