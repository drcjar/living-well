Change the rules, not just yourself

The biggest health gains come from changing the world we all share — its rules and surroundings — not from each person trying harder alone. As you grow up, your voice and your vote help decide that world.

Both clocks

Both clocks: this matters now, in the next ten years of your life, and over your whole life — roughly the next seventy years. More on the two clocks.

For ages 8–11

Every page here says the same quiet thing: living well is less about being strong than about the world around you being set up to help. Here is the biggest version of that — the world around you is built by people's decisions, and it can be built to make health easy or hard.

Whether the air indoors is clean, whether there are safe paths to walk and cycle on, whether the cheap and easy food is the healthy kind — none of that is fixed by nature. People decided it, through rules and choices, and people can decide it differently.

That is the hopeful part: it is not set in stone, and one day it will partly be up to you. When you are older you get a vote and a voice, and using them to help build a healthier world is one of the most powerful things you can do — for yourself and for everyone else.

For ages 11–16

The whole site argues one point: change your surroundings, not your willpower. But someone builds those surroundings — and before long, that someone includes you.

The biggest health lever isn't only your own habits; it's the rules and the surroundings everyone shares. Real examples that worked: banning smoking indoors, a minimum price on alcohol, the "sugar tax" that made drinks less sweet, safer routes to cycle. None of these asked anyone to try harder — they changed the defaults for everyone.

So here is the honest, empowering version, and it is the opposite of "not my problem": as you grow up, getting involved and voting for governments and representatives who make healthy choices easier becomes one of the most powerful health decisions you can make. Not to blame yourself for the world you inherited — but to use the say you are about to get, for yourself and the people around you.

For ages 16+

This page is the logical endpoint of everything else here — and it points at your ballot. If how much people smoke, drink, eat and move is powerfully shaped by their environment, then the highest-leverage health action is often to change the environment, and for a whole population that means policy, decided collectively.

The evidence is strongest exactly where the measure changes the environment and the study design is clean. Scotland's indoor smoking ban was followed by a sharp, repeated fall in heart-attack admissions, most of it in non-smokers who had merely been breathing the air. Its minimum unit price for alcohol was followed by a marked reduction in deaths caused by alcohol, concentrated in the most deprived communities. The UK's soft-drinks levy made manufacturers take the sugar out of their drinks, and was followed by a targeted reduction in childhood obesity in the poorest areas. (The precise figures, with sources, are in the evidence below.) Be honest about the weaker case, too: active-travel infrastructure — cycle paths, safe routes — is suggestive, not proven.

Put those together and an honest conclusion follows: the biggest population health improvements have generally come from changing the shared environment, not from urging individuals to try harder — so engaging as a citizen, and voting for representatives who prioritise health-promoting environments over ill-health-promoting ones, is itself a high-leverage, evidence-based health act. This is a reference, not a campaign, so it stops there: the evidence for what works is above; which measures are worth their trade-offs, and who best represents them, is a question of values that is yours to answer, not ours.

The evidence

This part is the same for every age: it is where the facts above come from. Each study is described in plain words first, then named so you can look it up. Stuck on a word? See Words we use.

Smoke-free laws

  • Scotland's indoor smoking ban. After Scotland banned smoking in enclosed public places in 2006, hospital admissions for heart attacks and similar heart emergencies (acute coronary syndrome) fell 17% — far more than in England over the same period — and about two-thirds of the fall was in non-smokers. Pell et al., NEJM 2008;359:482.
  • The pattern repeats elsewhere. Pooling 45 studies of 33 smoke-free laws, places with complete bans had fewer hospital admissions for heart attacks, strokes and breathing problems — and the more complete the ban, the bigger the effect. Meta-analysis: Tan & Glantz, Circulation 2012;126:2177.
  • And again. A Cochrane review of 77 studies found the same consistent heart benefit, clearest for fewer heart-attack admissions. Frazer et al. (Cochrane), smoke-free legislation review, 2016.

Minimum unit pricing (alcohol)

  • Scotland's minimum price for alcohol. After Scotland set a floor price of £0.50 per unit, deaths caused entirely by alcohol fell 13.4% compared with what would otherwise have been expected — most in the poorest areas — with a smaller fall in hospital admissions. Wyper et al., Lancet 2023;401:1361.
  • People bought less. Minimum unit pricing cut the amount of alcohol bought in shops and supermarkets by about 7.6%, mostly among the households that bought the most. O'Donnell et al., BMJ 2019;366:l5274.

Soft drinks industry levy ("sugar tax")

  • The sugar came out. After the levy was announced, drink makers changed their recipes: the share of drinks with enough sugar to be taxed fell by about 34 percentage points. Scarborough et al., PLoS Med 2020;17:e1003025.
  • Some children benefited. Among girls in Year 6 (age 10–11), obesity was about 1.6 percentage points lower than expected without the levy, most in the poorest areas; no change was seen in boys or younger children. Rogers et al., PLoS Med 2023;20:e1004160.

Active travel (weaker evidence)

  • New walking and cycling routes. After new traffic-free routes were built (the Connect2 scheme), people living nearer a route were walking and cycling more at two years — but there was no effect at one year, and some of the extra trips may have replaced other exercise rather than car journeys. Suggestive, not proof. Goodman et al., Am J Public Health 2014;104:e38.

The honest weakness

It is genuinely hard to prove that one policy caused a change across a whole population, and none of these are randomised trials. The strongest cases (smoke-free laws, minimum unit pricing) rest on well-designed natural experiments — careful before-and-after comparisons against places or groups the policy did not reach — with clear effects that have been repeated. The sugar levy's effect on recipes is unambiguous; its effect on health is real but targeted — some groups, not all — rather than universal. Active-travel infrastructure is the weakest link: plausible and supported, but not on the same footing. And the leap from "these measures work" to "vote this way" is deliberately left to you: the evidence can tell you what changes health, but weighing it against liberty, cost and other priorities — and judging who would deliver it — is a question of values, not data.
